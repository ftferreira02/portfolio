## The problem

MEMORIA started as a lifelog retrieval system built at the University of Aveiro for the
Lifelog Search Challenge (LSC). In the competition, trained operators search a
shared collection of wearable-camera images that was indexed long before the session starts.

Everyday use is different. People bring their own photos, wait for expensive analysis to finish, and
usually remember only part of a moment: who was there, roughly where, what happened. Rarely a date or a
file name. My thesis asked what it takes to turn a strong competition system into an application people
can use on their own libraries, and then built it on top of the existing retrieval foundation instead of
replacing it.

## What I built

I replaced the old client with a Next.js app and extended the Django backend for uploads, organisation,
people and conversational search. All models run on the MEMORIA server, so no photo is sent to an
outside service.

### Photos are searchable before analysis finishes

Captioning, OCR, object and scene detection and face analysis are slow, so I rebuilt ingestion around
Celery and Redis with persistent jobs in PostgreSQL. A photo becomes searchable as soon as its CLIP
embedding is in Milvus; the rest of the enrichment arrives later, and the interface shows per-photo
progress over WebSockets. Jobs are retried, and a recovery sweep picks up work that a restart
interrupted.

On a 200-photo upload with face recognition enabled, the first photo was searchable after 11 seconds and
all of them after about 12 minutes. Full analysis took about 12 seconds per photo, and all 1,600 jobs
succeeded on the first attempt.

### Days, Trips and Homes

Photos are grouped into Days automatically. Homes are estimated from photo locations, and deterministic
rules turn runs of days away from home into Trips (at least two dates, at most 30, with gaps bridged
only between days that have supporting places). Owners can rename a Trip and change its Days, and those
corrections are kept when the organisation is recomputed after new uploads.

### People, with consent

Face recognition is off until the owner enables it. Faces are grouped automatically, the owner names a
group once, and those confirmed references are used to match new photos. Groups can be renamed,
merged, split and corrected. Named people then become
something you can search by.

### Conversational search

You describe a moment in a sentence. Trips, Albums and people are matched first, then a local language
model (Qwen2.5-7B, served by Ollama) proposes what to look for and which constraints apply. The proposal is
grounded and validated, and shown back as constraints you can edit. If the model is unavailable or slow,
rules handle the message.

### Hybrid retrieval that respects the filters

In the inherited system, filters only restricted the full-text results, and visual search returned a
global top 100 with no owner filter. I changed retrieval so both sources (CLIP vectors in Milvus and
full-text in PostgreSQL) are restricted to the owner and the constraints before ranking, every photo is
checked again, and the two lists are fused with reciprocal rank fusion. Each owner only ever sees their
own photos, and automated tests check that separation.

## Results

**User study.** Ten participants uploaded their own libraries of 150 to 534 photos and looked for
moments in them.

- All 120 tasks ended with the moment found within the three-minute limit.
- The mean System Usability Scale score was **91.5**.
- Eight of the ten ranked Search first among the three ways of looking (Search, Memories and Photos).

**Rules versus the model.** On first descriptions, the rules were as accurate as the language model or
more so. The model earned its place mainly in refinements, when the owner adjusts a search that's
already on screen.

**Lifelog Search Challenge 2026.** I wrote the system paper,
[*MEMORIA: A Memory Enhancement and MOment RetrIeval Application at the LSC2026*](https://doi.org/10.1145/3810984.3817065)
(ACM, LSC '26), and competed with the retrieval foundation and an early version of the
natural-language interface. The competition console still runs on the same retrieval core as the new app.

## What I'd do next

The study also exposed requests where an unsupported part was dropped without notice. That is the
first fix: decline an unsupported part openly, so the owner can see and correct it. After that, I'd
test sending first descriptions to the rules and keeping the model for refinements, add an
owner-controlled profile for relationships ("my sister"), and run a larger study with libraries of
thousands of photos and people outside my own circle.
